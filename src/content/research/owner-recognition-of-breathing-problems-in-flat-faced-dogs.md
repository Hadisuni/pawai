## Plain-English summary

Flat-faced breeds snort, snuffle and snore, and that sound is so characteristic that many owners file it under personality rather than health. A 2015 study tested that assumption directly: it measured French Bulldogs' breathing objectively, graded how affected they were, and then compared that grading with what their owners believed.

Among the dogs the grading identified as clinically affected, 60% of owners had not recognised a problem. The noise had been normalised — heard, but not counted as something to raise.

This is not a study about negligent owners. It is a study about how hard it is to notice a problem that has been present for as long as you have known the animal.

## What the study found

Eighty-nine French Bulldogs drawn from the pet population, plus twenty non-brachycephalic control dogs, underwent whole-body barometric plethysmography — a non-invasive method that records breathing in an unsedated dog inside a measurement chamber, allowing repeated quantitative readings.

Each dog also received a respiratory functional grade based on observed signs such as respiratory noise and effort, assessed both before and after exercise. Grades 0 and I were treated as having insignificant clinical signs; Grades II and III as having significant signs.

The comparison between owner perception and functional grading is the finding most relevant to anyone living with one of these dogs: 60% of owners failed to recognise the syndrome in dogs that graded as significantly affected.

The airflow measurements themselves separated the groups clearly. Traces differed significantly between affected and unaffected French Bulldogs — and, notably, between non-brachycephalic controls and French Bulldogs graded 0, the least affected grade. The researchers used these parameters to build a classifier and derive a BOAS Index, which reached a sensitivity of 0.97 and specificity of 0.93 in the training group of 69 dogs, and 0.95 accuracy in a separate test group of 20.

## What this study cannot tell you

The population was specific and small: 89 French Bulldogs at a single centre. Other flat-faced breeds — Pugs, Bulldogs, Boston Terriers — were not assessed here, and the authors present broader application as a possibility rather than a result.

The 60% figure is a single measurement in a single study group. It is a strong signal that normalisation is widespread; it is not a precise national rate, and it should not be quoted as one.

The classifier's validation set was 20 dogs. Those accuracy figures are promising, not settled.

And the finding that Grade 0 French Bulldogs differed from non-brachycephalic controls needs care in the telling. It says something about the breed's airway as measured on this equipment. It does not mean every French Bulldog is ill, and it is not a basis for concluding anything about your own dog.

## What pet owners should track

The useful shift is from *hearing* the noise to *recording* it, in terms a veterinarian can act on:

- Breathing noise at rest, and whether it has changed over months — a phone video captures this far better than a description
- How your dog breathes after ordinary exertion, and how long it takes to settle back down
- Whether your dog struggles in heat, or seeks cool surfaces more than it used to
- How your dog sleeps: position, restlessness, waking, whether it seems to fight for breath
- Whether it stops or slows on walks it used to complete comfortably

Record these against dates. A slow drift is invisible day to day and obvious across six months of notes.

Breathing difficulty, blue-tinged gums, or collapse are emergencies. Contact a veterinarian immediately — do not observe and record.
