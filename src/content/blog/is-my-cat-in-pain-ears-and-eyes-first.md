> **If you think your cat is in pain, contact your veterinarian.** Nothing on this page is a reason to watch and wait. If your cat is breathing hard at rest, can't use its back legs, or is straining without passing urine, that's an emergency — call a clinic now.

"Cats hide pain" is one of the most repeated lines in pet care. It's partly true, and it leaves a lot of owners feeling they couldn't tell even if they tried.

A 2023 study tested that directly, on a large scale — and the results are more encouraging than the saying suggests.

## What the study did

Researchers ran an online survey in English and Spanish between March and May 2021, shared through International Cat Care and other channels. Of 3,039 responses, **1,262 were complete, from 66 countries**. Participants were adult cat caregivers who were not veterinary professionals.

Each person scored **10 photographs** of cats' faces showing different levels of pain, using the **Feline Grimace Scale** — a structured tool that looks at five parts of the face:

1. Ear position
2. Orbital tightening (how tight or squinted the eyes are)
3. Muzzle tension
4. Whiskers change
5. Head position

Eight veterinarians scored the same images.

## What it found

- **Owners' total scores were not significantly different from the vets'.** The reliability of those totals was rated *good*.
- The only feature where owners differed was **muzzle tension**, which they scored slightly higher than vets.
- Some features were much steadier signals than others:
  - **Ear position** and **eye tightening** were the most reliable (both in the study's "good" band).
  - **Muzzle tension** was moderate.
  - **Whiskers** and **head position** were the weakest.
- Age, country and other background details didn't significantly change how people scored.

In plain terms: when owners were given a clear checklist, they read pain in a cat's face about as consistently as vets did — and the ears and eyes were where they were most consistent.

## What this doesn't mean

It's worth being honest about the limits, because they matter for how you use this:

- **These were unusually engaged owners.** People who answer a survey shared by a cat-welfare charity aren't a random sample.
- **Photos are easier than real life.** A still, well-framed image isn't the same as reading your own cat across a dim room while it hides or reacts to you.
- **It measured agreement, not outcomes.** It shows people can apply the scale consistently, not that home scoring leads to earlier or better care.
- **The scale is for acute pain** — sudden, recent pain. Slow, long-term discomfort (like arthritis) is outside what was studied, and often shows up as behaviour changes instead.

So this isn't a home pain test, and it isn't a reason to manage pain yourself. It's a better way to *notice* and *describe*.

## Where to look, in order

When something seems off, look at the face before you decide "they're just grumpy":

1. **Ears** — held normally, or rotated outward and flattened?
2. **Eyes** — open and relaxed, or tightened and squinted?
3. **Muzzle** — relaxed, or drawn tight around the mouth and nose?
4. **Head** — carried level with the shoulders, or held low?
5. **Whiskers** — note them, but weigh them least.

Then write down:

- **When you first noticed it**
- **Whether it comes and goes or stays**
- **What else changed** — hiding, eating, litter box, jumping, grooming

**Take a short video if you can.** Facial signs are easy to miss in a single moment and easy to doubt later; a 10–20 second clip is often more useful to your vet than any description.

## When not to wait

Contact your vet the same day if your cat's face looks pained and it isn't settling, or if it's paired with not eating, hiding, or a change in toileting. Treat these as emergencies and call a clinic now:

- Breathing hard, fast or open-mouthed while resting
- Sudden pain or weakness in the back legs
- Straining in the litter box with little or no urine
