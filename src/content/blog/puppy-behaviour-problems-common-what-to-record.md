## "Everyone else's dog seems fine"

Raising a young dog can feel lonely. The puppy at the park walks nicely on the lead, the neighbour's dog never chews anything, and yours jumps up, pulls, barks at the door or falls apart when you leave the room. It's easy to conclude that something has gone wrong, and that it's your fault.

A large UK study suggests a different starting point: difficult behaviour in young dogs is the common experience, not the exception.

## What the research found

A 2024 study in the journal *Animals* followed **985 dogs** from the UK "Pandemic Puppies" cohort, puppies acquired during 2020, to **21 months of age**. The original survey of 4,369 puppies had recorded how and why each was acquired and what socialisation and habituation they had before 16 weeks. The follow-up asked owners about behaviour, management and training up to 21 months.

- **96.7% of owners** reported at least one problem behaviour by 21 months. The median was **five** (most owners reported between three and seven).
- **30.9% of dogs** were reported to show separation-related behaviours.
- **82.3% of owners** had used at least one aversive training technique, such as physical punishment, which the authors describe as notably high compared with previous studies.
- Owners who reported more problem behaviours were more likely to have used multiple aversive techniques.
- **Attending online puppy classes** was the only factor found to be protective against owners using aversive methods.
- Almost a third of owners had **underestimated how hard training would be**, more often first-time owners.

The authors call for urgent support for this group of dogs, including owner education on humane training.

## What it can't tell you

Problem behaviours and aversive training were measured together, at one time, by the same person. The study can't say which came first. A difficult dog might lead to harsher handling, harsher handling might make behaviour worse, or both might feed each other. It shows they travel together, nothing more.

The same goes for online puppy classes. Owners who sign up for classes may differ in other ways, so the study can't separate the class from the kind of owner who attends one.

Everything was reported by owners, including what counts as a "problem". Part of that 96.7% reflects what owners were willing to call a problem.

These were puppies acquired during the 2020 pandemic, a group already known to have faced more risk factors: restricted socialisation, unusual purchasing routes and many first-time owners. Their results aren't a baseline for all dogs. And only 985 of the original 4,369 owners took part in the follow-up, so those who stayed may not be typical.

Finally, behaviour change can have a **medical cause, including pain**. The study doesn't address that, and neither can a blog post. A veterinarian can.

## Signs that can't wait

Most behaviour worries are a reason to book an appointment, not an emergency. But some situations need a vet straight away.

### Call a vet or emergency clinic now

- Your dog is **injured**, for example bleeding or limping after a fight with another dog, or after an incident during handling
- Your dog **may have swallowed something** they chewed (a sock, toy, stuffing, wood or plastic), especially with **repeated vomiting or retching**, a **swollen or tight belly**, or obvious discomfort
- Your dog is **collapsed, can't stand, is struggling to breathe, or has a seizure**
- **Sudden aggression together with clear signs of pain**, such as yelping or snapping when a particular area is touched

If a person has been bitten, get them medical care first.

### Same-day or next-day vet

- A **sudden change** in behaviour with no obvious reason, such as a calm dog that becomes irritable or withdrawn over a few days
- Behaviour changes **alongside physical signs**: limping, reluctance to move or jump, changes in eating, drinking or toileting
- New **toileting accidents** indoors from a dog that had been reliably house-trained

### Worth booking an appointment

- One or more behaviours that are **getting worse over weeks**, or not improving despite your efforts
- **Distress when left alone**, or reports of barking or damage while you're out
- You're finding training hard and want help, including a referral to a **qualified, welfare-focused behaviourist**

Your vet is the right first stop for behaviour worries, because they can check for pain and medical causes before training is the focus.

## What to keep in your dog's record

The questions you'll be asked later ("what did they meet as a puppy? when did this start? is it getting better?") are exactly the ones nobody writes down at the time.

- **Early experiences** in the first 16 weeks: what and who your puppy met, and **how they reacted**, not just that it happened
- **Any behaviour you find difficult**, with the **date it started** and what was going on around it
- Whether each behaviour is **better, worse or the same** from week to week
- **Behaviour when left alone**, since nearly a third of dogs in this study showed separation-related behaviours and most of it happens out of sight
- **Training approaches** you've tried, who in the household uses them, and **what actually works**
- **Classes attended**, and by which family members

Take these notes to your vet or behaviourist. They're for a professional to interpret, not for diagnosing your dog yourself.
