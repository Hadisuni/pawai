## The question nobody wants to ask

Your dog is older now. Walks are shorter, the stairs take longer, and some nights are restless. Somewhere in the back of your mind is a question you'd rather not look at directly: *how will I know when?*

There is no formula, and this post won't pretend there is. But it can help to know what other families have actually been through, and it can help to have something steadier than memory when the time comes to talk it through with your vet.

## What the research found

A 2026 study in the *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association* used the Dog Aging Project's End of Life Survey. Owners whose dogs had died between December 2019 and March 2021 were invited to describe how their dog died. Of 793 invited, 655 responded (85.7%, an unusually high rate), and **646 responses** were analysed.

- The average age at death was **13.0 years**.
- **83.0% of dogs** (536 of 646) were **euthanased** rather than dying unassisted. About three in four of those euthanasias took place in a veterinary clinic and just over one in five at home.
- **14.7% of dogs** (95 of 646) died **without any veterinary involvement**.
- The causes of death owners reported most often were **cancer (29.7%)**, **"old age" (29.4%)** and **organ system diseases (22.3%)**.
- The reason owners gave most often for euthanasia was **pain and/or suffering (48.5%)**, followed by **poor quality of life (24.8%)**.
- The older the dog, the **poorer the owner-rated quality of life** near the end.

In other words, for most families the decision wasn't made on a diagnosis alone. It was made on their sense of how their dog was feeling day to day.

## What it can't tell you

Everything in this study is what owners perceived. "Cause of death" is what the owner believed, not what a post-mortem found, and "old age" describes an ending rather than explaining it.

The survey's own validation study is the most important caveat. Owners' answers agreed well for cause of death, only moderately for quality of life near the end, and only fairly for the reason for euthanasia. So the headline 48.5% figure for pain and suffering rests on the least reliable question in the survey. Read it as what owners describe, not as a precise measurement.

These were volunteer families in a research project who chose to answer questions about their dog's death. Owners who found that too painful aren't represented. The average age of 13 years also pools dogs of every size, and life expectancy varies a lot with body size, so it isn't a prediction for your dog.

Most of all, the study is descriptive. It doesn't tell anyone *when*, and it doesn't compare approaches to end-of-life care. That conversation belongs with your veterinary team.

## Signs that can't wait

Slow decline over months is something to talk through calmly with your vet. Some situations are different and need a vet straight away, at any age.

### Call a vet or emergency clinic now

- Your dog is **struggling to breathe**, breathing fast at rest, or has pale, grey or blue gums
- Your dog has **collapsed, can't stand**, or has a **seizure**
- Your dog seems to be in **pain you can't settle**: crying out, panting and restless, unable to lie down comfortably
- **Repeated vomiting**, a **swollen or tight belly**, or retching that brings nothing up
- **Bleeding** that won't stop, or sudden severe weakness

If your dog may be dying at home and is distressed, call your vet or an emergency clinic. In this study, nearly one dog in seven died with no vet involved, and nobody should have to manage that moment alone.

### Same-day or next-day vet

- **Not eating** for more than a day, or not drinking
- A clear **change over a few days**: much weaker, sleeping far more, no longer getting up to greet you
- **New accidents indoors**, or straining to pass urine or stool
- A new lump that is **growing quickly** or a wound that isn't healing

### Worth booking an appointment

- You've noticed a **gradual decline** and want to talk about what's ahead
- You're wondering whether your dog is **comfortable**, or whether current medicines still help
- You'd like to understand your options, including **timing, home visits and what to expect**

Please don't give human painkillers or leftover medicines to "help them through the night." Several are dangerous for dogs. Call your vet instead.

Asking early is not giving up and it isn't premature. Your veterinary team can help with the question of timing too.

## What to keep in your dog's record

Decline is gradual, and memory tends to hold onto the last few hard days. A simple dated record gives you and your vet a trajectory instead of a snapshot.

- **Mobility**: getting up, stairs, slipping, and whether your dog still *chooses* to move
- **Appetite and drinking**, and whether they now need encouragement
- **Toileting**, including accidents in a dog who was always clean
- **Sleep**: restlessness, pacing or panting at night
- **Connection**: whether your dog still seeks you out and still enjoys the things they always loved
- **Good days and bad days**, simply counted. The pattern over weeks says more than any single day
- **Every medicine**, its dose, and whether it still seems to help
- If your vet gives you a **quality-of-life scale**, use that one and write down each score with its date, so you're comparing like with like

None of this decides anything. It's there so the conversation with your vet starts from what has really been happening.
