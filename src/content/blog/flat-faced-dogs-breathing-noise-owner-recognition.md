## The calm version first

Snorting, snuffling, and snoring are so common in flat-faced breeds that many households stop hearing them as a health signal. That is not carelessness. It is how the ear adapts to a sound that has been present since the first week with the dog.

PAWai’s job here is peacemind: separate **normalised noise** from **change and urgency**, so you know whether to keep a dated record, call same-day, or go now — without diagnosing airway disease at home.

| Read | What it usually means for you |
|---|---|
| **Routine / monitor** | Stable noise you’ve already logged; dog bright, recovers quickly after walks — keep dated notes and videos |
| **Same-day** | Noise or recovery time is worse than your baseline, heat intolerance is new, sleep is restless — call your clinic today |
| **Urgent** | Marked effort at rest, struggling after mild exercise, gums look off, or you’re unsure — do not wait overnight |
| **Emergency** | Collapse, blue-tinged gums, severe distress, inability to settle breathing — emergency care now |

Nothing below replaces a veterinarian’s judgment for *your* dog.

## What one study actually measured

PAWai’s research card summarises a 2015 *PLOS ONE* study of French Bulldogs that compared **owner perception** with **objective airway grading** (including measurements before and after exercise).

Among dogs the grading identified as clinically affected, **60% of owners had not recognised a problem**. The noise had been normalised — heard, but not counted as something to raise.

Full card (plain-English summary, limits, citation):  
[Six in Ten Owners Did Not Recognise Breathing Trouble…](https://www.pawai.it.com/research/owner-recognition-of-breathing-problems-in-flat-faced-dogs)

### Limits that matter (don’t overclaim)

- One breed (French Bulldogs), one centre, modest sample — not a national rate for all flat-faced dogs.
- Pugs, English Bulldogs, Boston Terriers were not assessed in that cohort.
- Home observation is not the laboratory test used in the paper. You cannot “confirm BOAS” with a phone video; you *can* bring a clearer history.

## Why this fits triage / peacemind

Urgency is not only “is something new tonight?” It is also “has something drifted so slowly that I stopped noticing?”

Breathing noise that never changes in your memory can still be clinically relevant — and **worsening** recovery after walks, heat struggle, or sleep fighting for breath is the pattern clinics need to hear.

## What to track before the visit

Shift from “he’s always snorty” to dated evidence:

1. **At-rest breathing** — short phone video, same room/distance each time  
2. **After ordinary exertion** — how long until breathing settles  
3. **Heat** — seeking cool floors, cutting walks short, distress in warm weather  
4. **Sleep** — position, restlessness, waking, effort  
5. **Walk stamina** — routes the dog used to finish without stopping  

Pair each note with a date. Six months of quiet drift is hard to feel day to day and easy to show on a timeline.

## When not to wait and record

Breathing difficulty that looks severe, blue-tinged gums, collapse, or an animal that cannot settle — **contact emergency veterinary care immediately**. Observation checklists are for non-emergency monitoring, not for delaying care.

---

## Bottom line

“Normal for the breed” is a cultural phrase, not a medical clearance. One study showed how often owners of affected French Bulldogs had not flagged a problem. Your useful move is quieter than panic and sharper than dismissal: **record change (or stability) with dates, then let a veterinarian interpret it.**
