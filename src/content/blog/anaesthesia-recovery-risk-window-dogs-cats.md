## The calm version first

“Will my pet wake up?” is the fear most owners bring to a dental, spay, or imaging under anaesthetic. Reassurance is common. Numbers are rarer. Timing is rarer still.

One large UK enquiry found that anaesthetic-related deaths were **not concentrated only during the procedure**. A large share happened **afterward** — in the recovery period the study followed out to 48 hours. That does **not** mean going home is unsafe. It means the discharge instructions, the out-of-hours number, and the first two days of eating, drinking, breathing, and alertness are part of the safety story — not an afterthought.

PAWai’s job here is peacemind: separate **fear of the machine** from **what the evidence says about when attention still matters**, so you leave with a clear record and know when to call — without pretending a blog post can score risk for your animal.

| Read | What it usually means for you |
|---|---|
| **Routine / monitor** | Discharge instructions followed; eating/drinking/eliminating as the clinic described; alertness improving — keep a short dated recovery log |
| **Same-day** | Recovery slower than the clinic’s expected range, mild but persistent flatness, or something in the discharge sheet that no longer matches what you’re seeing — call the practice today |
| **Urgent** | Laboured breathing, will not settle, refuses food/water when they said that should return, or clear deterioration after coming home — do not wait overnight |
| **Emergency** | Collapse, severe distress, inability to breathe comfortably, or rapid decline — emergency veterinary care now |

Nothing below replaces a veterinarian’s judgment for *your* pet. Anaesthetic and surgical risk depend on health status, age, procedure, and the team — none of which a population percentage can supply.

## What one large study found

The Confidential Enquiry into Perioperative Small Animal Fatalities (CEPSAF) was a prospective multicentre cohort study with a nested case-control component. Practices recorded animals anaesthetised or sedated between June 2002 and June 2004; outcomes were recorded at **48 hours**. An anaesthetic-related death was one where surgical or pre-existing medical causes did not solely account for it.

Across **117** UK practices the study covered **98,036** dogs, **79,178** cats, and **8,209** rabbits.

Overall risk of anaesthetic- and sedation-related death within 48 hours was about:

- **0.17%** in dogs (**1 in 601**)
- **0.24%** in cats (**1 in 419**)
- **1.39%** in rabbits (**1 in 72**)

Health status separated those figures sharply. In healthier animals (ASA grades 1–2), risks were about **1 in 1,849** dogs and **1 in 895** cats. In sicker animals (ASA 3–5), risks rose to about **1 in 75** dogs and **1 in 71** cats.

**Timing** is the peacemind takeaway: postoperative deaths accounted for about **47%** of anaesthetic-related deaths in dogs, **61%** in cats, and **64%** in rabbits. The authors concluded that greater care in the postoperative period could reduce fatalities.

## What this study cannot tell you

- Data were collected in **2002–2004**. Drugs, monitoring, and protocols have moved. The authors themselves framed findings against a trend of improving safety.
- More recent worldwide studies reported **higher** anaesthesia-related mortality figures — but used different sampling methods and list selection bias among their limitations. Quoting a single “risk of anaesthesia” number oversimplifies.
- Participating practices volunteered; UK centres willing to be audited may not represent every clinic everywhere.
- Classifying a death as anaesthetic-related is a clinical judgment, not a mechanical tag.
- These are **population** figures. They do **not** estimate risk for your animal.

## What to note before and after you talk to your vet

Most of the actionable value is at **discharge** and in the **first 48 hours**:

1. **ASA / health status grade** — ask and write it down if they share it; in this study it was the strongest separator of risk groups.
2. **Discharge sheet as given** — what is normal, what is not, when eating/drinking should return, and the **out-of-hours number**.
3. **Eating, drinking, urinating, toileting** — times, not vibes, for the first couple of days.
4. **Breathing and alertness** — especially overnight after coming home.
5. **Medications at home** — each dose and time.
6. **Prior anaesthetic history** — which year, any complications; this is asked every time and hard to reconstruct from memory.

For packing the visit story: [Why preparing before a vet visit helps](https://www.pawai.it.com/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-vet-visit).

## When not to “watch and log”

Unusually flat demeanour, laboured breathing, inability to settle, vomiting with clear decline, or deterioration after discharge are **not** journaling problems. Contact the practice promptly — or emergency care if your pet is in severe distress. The study’s central message is that recovery is part of the risk window; out-of-hours numbers exist for a reason.
