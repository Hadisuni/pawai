## The problem that hides in plain sight

Your dog's breath has been a bit worse lately. Maybe they've started chewing on one side, dropping bits of kibble, or pulling their head away when you touch their muzzle. None of it looks urgent, and most dogs keep eating and wagging, so it's easy to let it slide until the next routine visit.

Mouths are easy to ignore. They're not pleasant to look at, and dogs rarely complain about them. That's exactly why a few dated notes and a photo can make such a difference at the vet.

## What the research found

A 2021 study in the *Journal of Small Animal Practice* used VetCompass, a project that collects anonymised records from UK primary-care vet clinics. It looked at a random sample of **22,333 dogs** seen during 2016.

- **12.52%** had periodontal disease (gum disease) diagnosed and recorded within that single year. That's about **one dog in eight**.
- **Breed mattered.** Eighteen breeds had higher odds than crossbred dogs. The highest were the Toy Poodle (**3.97 times** the odds), King Charles Spaniel (2.63), Greyhound (2.58), and Cavalier King Charles Spaniel (2.39).
- **Spaniel types** had **1.63 times** the odds of non-spaniel types, and **flat-faced (brachycephalic) breeds** had **1.25 times** the odds of breeds with a medium-length muzzle.
- **Smaller dogs carried more risk.** As adult bodyweight went up, the odds went steadily down, which is the opposite of what many owners expect.

Here's the striking part. A 2020 review of the wider research found that when vets look at a dog's mouth while the dog is awake, as in a normal check-up, they find gum disease in roughly **9% to 18%** of dogs. When dogs are under anaesthetic and the whole mouth, including below the gumline, is examined properly, studies report **44% to 100%**.

Both numbers are honest. The gap between them is the disease that exists but hasn't been found yet.

## What it can't tell you

The 12.52% counts diagnoses written in clinical notes, not disease actually present in mouths. A dog with early gum disease that nobody looked at closely is counted as not having it.

The breed results may partly reflect who gets checked. Owners of small breeds, or breeds known for dental trouble, may bring them in more often or have dental checks raised sooner, which can make those groups look more affected.

The study looked at UK dogs in 2016 and didn't test any prevention. Nothing here shows whether brushing, diets, chews, or how often a dog has a professional cleaning makes a difference. The 44% to 100% range also comes from studies with different dogs and grading methods, so it's a sense of scale, not a precise figure.

## Signs that can't wait

### Call a vet or emergency clinic now

- **Sudden swelling of the face**, jaw, or under one eye
- **Bleeding from the mouth** that doesn't stop
- A **broken tooth** after an injury, especially if you can see pink or red in the middle of it
- Your dog **can't close their mouth**, or is pawing at it in obvious distress
- **Not eating or drinking** together with mouth pain, drooling, or lethargy
- Any **injury to the mouth or jaw**, or something stuck between the teeth or across the roof of the mouth

### Same-day or next-day vet

- Breath that has become **much worse quite suddenly**
- **Chewing on one side**, dropping food, or crying out while eating
- A tooth that **looks loose** or has turned a darker colour
- Your dog suddenly **won't let you touch their face** or muzzle

### Worth booking an appointment

- Breath that has **gradually got worse** over weeks or months
- **Visible tartar** or redness along the gumline
- Your dog is in a **higher-risk group**: small breed, spaniel type, flat-faced, or simply getting older, and you can't remember their last dental assessment

Bad breath on its own isn't a diagnosis, and fresh breath isn't a guarantee that all is well. Only a vet can assess gum disease, and the thorough version of that check happens under anaesthetic.

## What to keep in your dog's record

- **When the breath changed**, and whether it's getting worse. A change is more useful than "it's always been a bit bad"
- **Eating changes**: chewing on one side, dropping food, slower meals, or preferring soft food
- **Handling changes**: pulling away when you touch the head or muzzle
- **A clear phone photo** of the teeth and gumline, taken in good light, for comparison over time
- **The date of the last dental assessment** and what was found or done
- **Your dog's risk group**, written down honestly, so it's easy to mention

Bring these up yourself rather than waiting to be asked, especially if your dog is small, a spaniel type, flat-faced, or older.
