## The part of your dog's day you never see

You close the door, and for the next few hours your dog's life happens without you. What you know about it comes in pieces: a chewed door frame, a puddle by the door, a neighbour who mentions the barking, the way your dog greets you when you get back.

From those pieces, most owners build a picture. Maybe "they're fine once I've gone," or "I think they panic." It's natural to wonder how close that picture is to what really happens, and one small study set out to check.

## What the research found

A 2018 study in the *Journal of Veterinary Behavior* compared two things for **31 dogs**: what their owners reported on the separation section of a standard behaviour questionnaire (the C-BARQ), and **video of what each dog actually did in the first 25 minutes after the owner left**. Some dogs were filmed with one or two other dogs at home; the rest were alone.

- Owner questionnaire scores showed a **significant but moderate** match with the share of time dogs spent in behaviours suggestive of anxiety on video (rs = 0.438).
- A similar moderate match held against an overall summary score of the recorded behaviours (rs = 0.385).
- Owners were **most accurate for obvious behaviours, such as destruction**: the things that leave evidence to come home to. Agreement was weaker for subtler signs.

The authors' conclusion is carefully hedged: owners may have *indicative* knowledge of what their dog does while they're away, but more work with more dogs and repeated recordings is needed.

In plain terms: you're not in the dark, but you're not seeing the full picture either.

## What it can't tell you

Thirty-one dogs is a small group, and each dog was filmed only once. Dogs have good days and bad days, and a single session can't separate a dog's usual pattern from how it behaved one afternoon with a new camera in the room.

Twenty-five minutes is a short window. It was a reasonable choice, since separation-related behaviour tends to show up early, but the study says nothing about the rest of a long working day.

A correlation of 0.438 means owner reports carry real signal and real error at the same time. It doesn't support "owners know," and it doesn't support "owners have no idea." It also can't tell you whether *your* impression of *your* dog is one of the accurate ones.

Some dogs had other dogs with them, which adds variation the study wasn't designed to untangle.

And importantly: behaviour when alone can overlap with medical causes, including **pain** and **cognitive decline in older dogs**. A video or a chewed door can't tell you which is going on. That's a job for a veterinarian or a qualified behaviourist, not something to diagnose at home.

## Signs that can't wait

Most home-alone worries are a reason to book an appointment, not an emergency. But sometimes what you come home to needs a vet straight away.

### Call a vet or emergency clinic now

- **Bleeding, a broken tooth, or an injured paw or mouth**, for example after chewing or scratching at a door, crate or window
- You think your dog **may have swallowed something** they chewed (fabric, stuffing, plastic, wood), especially with **repeated vomiting or retching**, a **swollen or tight belly**, or obvious discomfort
- Your dog is **collapsed, can't stand, or is struggling to breathe**
- Your dog **escaped or fell** while you were out and may have been hurt, even if they seem fine now

### Same-day or next-day vet

- A **sudden change** in how your dog behaves when left alone, with no obvious reason, especially in an **older dog**
- Home-alone changes along with **signs of pain**: limping, reluctance to move, yelping when touched, or not wanting to lie down
- New **toileting accidents** indoors from a dog that has been reliably house-trained

### Worth booking an appointment

- A **pattern** of damage, toileting or barking reports when your dog is left, even if each incident seems small
- Your dog becomes **very distressed as you get ready to leave**, or takes a long time to settle after you come home
- You'd like help understanding what's going on, with your notes and any recordings in hand

## What to keep in your dog's record

This is an area where writing things down adds something that paying close attention can't, because you simply aren't there.

- **What you come home to**: damage, toileting, disturbed bedding, an emptied water bowl, with the **date** and **how long you were out**
- **Neighbours' reports** of barking or howling, including the **time**
- **How your dog behaves in the minutes before you leave**, which you *can* watch directly
- **How your dog greets you**, and how long they take to settle afterwards
- If you film, **how long you were gone** and whether anything was **unusual about that departure**

Take your notes and recordings to your vet or a qualified behaviourist. Record for a professional to interpret, not to diagnose your dog yourself.
