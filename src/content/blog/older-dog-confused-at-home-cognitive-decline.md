## The changes that slip into "normal"

Your dog has lived in the same house for years, and now they stand facing a wall, wait at the hinge side of the door, or wander the hallway at 3 a.m. They may greet you less, sleep all afternoon, or have an accident indoors for the first time in a decade.

Because these changes arrive slowly, most of us file them under "he's just getting old." Sometimes that's true. Sometimes it's a recognised condition called canine cognitive dysfunction, and sometimes it's something else entirely, like pain or failing eyesight. A written record helps your vet tell those apart.

## What the research found

A 2022 study in *Scientific Reports* used the Dog Aging Project, a large nationwide cohort of companion dogs in the United States, to look at cognitive changes in **15,019 dogs**. Owners filled in a 13-question behaviour survey based on an established cognitive dysfunction rating scale. It asked how often their dog got stuck behind objects, paced, or failed to recognise familiar people.

- With everything else measured held equal, the odds of scoring as cognitive dysfunction rose **52% with each additional year of age**.
- Dogs described as **not active** had **6.47 times** the odds of dogs described as very active, among dogs of the same age, health status, breed type, and neuter status.
- Dogs with a history of **eye, ear, or neurological disorders** also had higher odds, roughly double for eye and ear problems.
- Across the whole group, whose average age was about 7, only **1.4%** scored above the cut-off. The share climbs steeply in older dogs.

## What it can't tell you

The activity finding is the one most likely to be misread. Activity and behaviour were measured at the same point in time, so the study can't show which came first. A dog whose thinking is changing may simply move less. Pain or arthritis could also drive both. The authors say this themselves. Exercise is good for older dogs for many reasons, but this study is not proof that walks prevent dementia.

No dog in the study was diagnosed by a vet. Cognitive status was a score from an owner survey, with no brain imaging or exam behind it. The survey also has a blind spot that matters at home: a dog who can't see or hear well may bump into doors, miss dropped food, or ignore their name, which looks like confusion on a checklist without being cognitive disease.

Finally, these were volunteer families enrolled in a research project, and the data were collected during 2020, when many households' routines had changed.

## Signs that can't wait

Gradual change over months is one thing. A sudden change over hours or a day or two is different, and it isn't a sign of normal ageing.

### Call a vet or emergency clinic now

- A **seizure**, collapse, or fainting
- **Sudden confusion, head tilt, circling, falling to one side**, or eyes flicking back and forth
- **Can't stand or walk**, or suddenly drags or knuckles over on the legs
- **Sudden blindness**, such as walking into things that weren't a problem yesterday
- **Crying out in pain**, or a swollen, hard belly with retching that brings nothing up
- **Hard to wake** or unresponsive

### Same-day or next-day vet

- Confusion or night-time restlessness that has **appeared or worsened within days**
- New house-soiling together with **drinking or urinating much more** than usual
- **Not eating** for more than a day, or a sudden change in appetite
- New **snapping or aggression** when touched or woken

### Worth booking an appointment

- Getting stuck behind furniture or **at the wrong side of doors**
- **Pacing or restlessness at night** with more sleeping by day
- Greeting family less, or seeming not to recognise familiar people
- **Accidents indoors** in a dog who was reliably house-trained
- Slower on walks, less interested in play, or reluctant on stairs

Please don't start supplements, calming products, or any human medicine to "see if it helps." Talk to your vet first, since several medicines and supplements interact with conditions older dogs commonly have.

## What to keep in your dog's record

- **What happened, with the date it started**, for example "first stood facing the wall, 2 Oct"
- **How often it happens now**: never, monthly, weekly, daily, or more than once a day. That is how the study's survey scored each behaviour
- **Time of day**, especially whether it's mostly at night
- **Sight and hearing**: does your dog react to a dropped treat, or to their name from another room?
- **Stiffness or pain**, such as slow to rise, avoiding stairs, licking joints
- **Water, appetite, and toilet changes** next to the behaviour notes
- **A short phone video** of the pacing or getting stuck, which can show your vet more than a description
