## The change you can't see by looking

You see your cat every day, so slow changes are the hardest ones to notice. An older cat who is eating well, maybe even asking for more food, and who still looks a normal shape rarely seems like a cat with a problem.

Weight loss in older cats often hides in exactly this way. A cat who started out on the heavy side can lose a lot and still land somewhere that looks "about right." Usually the scale is what notices first.

## What the research found

A 2016 study in the *Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine* assessed **462 cats with untreated hyperthyroidism**, one of the most common hormonal conditions in older cats. Each cat's weight, body condition (fat), and muscle condition were scored, and their current weight was compared with what their records showed one to two years earlier.

- Median weight had fallen from **5.45 kg** to **4.36 kg**, about a kilogram. For a cat that size, that's close to **a fifth of their body weight**.
- Yet only **35.3%** of the cats were assessed as thin or emaciated. Most still had an ideal or even overweight body condition.
- **77.3%** had lost muscle mass. The muscle picture was much worse than the fat picture.
- In 117 cats checked again 3 to 12 months after treatment, weight and both condition scores improved. Even so, mild to moderate muscle loss was still present in **45%** of them.

Put simply, the cat's look understated the change, and the number on the scale on its own understated the muscle loss.

## What it can't tell you

Every cat in this study already had a hyperthyroidism diagnosis. The study didn't follow healthy cats, and it didn't test whether weighing at home catches the disease earlier. A home scale is not a screening test for hyperthyroidism.

Weight and muscle loss also aren't specific to one condition. Kidney disease, diabetes, dental disease, cancer, and plain ageing can all cause them in older cats. Hyperthyroidism is diagnosed by a vet with a blood test. A falling weight is a reason to get your cat checked, not an answer about what's wrong.

The earlier weights came from ordinary clinic records rather than a fixed research schedule, and the follow-up group was only part of the original 462 cats. Treat those numbers as a useful signal rather than precise figures.

## Signs that can't wait

### Call a vet or emergency clinic now

- **Breathing fast or with effort** while resting, or **open-mouth breathing**
- **Sudden weakness, wobbling, or dragging of the back legs**, or crying out in pain
- **Collapse**, fainting, or being unusually hard to rouse
- **Sudden blindness**, such as bumping into things or very wide pupils that don't respond to light
- **Repeated vomiting**, or vomiting together with not eating
- **Not eating at all for a day or more**. In cats, going without food carries its own risks

### Same-day or next-day vet

- **Weight loss you've measured**, especially alongside a **bigger appetite than usual**
- **Drinking or urinating noticeably more** than normal
- New **restlessness, yowling at night**, or a racing heartbeat you can feel when your cat is calm

### Worth booking an appointment

- Your cat's **spine, hips, or shoulder blades feel bonier** under your hand, even though they still look fine
- A coat that has become **unkempt or greasy**
- Being more irritable or restless than they used to be
- More frequent **vomiting or looser stools** without other worrying signs

Please don't change your cat's food or give any human medicine to "see if it helps." Talk to your vet first.

## What to keep in your cat's record

- **A weight, with the date**, on the same scale every one to two months. A baby or pet scale is the most accurate; weighing yourself with and without your cat works if that's all you have
- **Keep the old numbers.** The study's whole comparison depended on a weight from one to two years earlier
- **Appetite next to the weight.** "Eating more but weighing less" is worth saying to your vet in those exact words
- **What you feel with your hands.** Note when the spine or hips start to feel more prominent
- **Water bowl and litter box changes**, such as more refills or bigger clumps
- **The body condition and muscle condition scores your vet gives**, because they're two separate assessments and the muscle one is easy to forget
