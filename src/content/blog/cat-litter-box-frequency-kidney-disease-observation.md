## The calm version first

Chronic kidney disease is common in older cats, and early signs are easy to normalize: drinking a bit more, eating a bit less, or “being quieter.” One place researchers actually measured an owner-visible difference is unglamorous — how often stools appear in the litter box.

A 2021 survey asked owners to clean the box daily for a week and write down what they found. Cats already diagnosed with chronic kidney disease passed stools less often than cats their owners considered healthy. Consistency of the stool did **not** separate the groups. Frequency did.

That does **not** mean a slow litter box equals kidney disease. About one in seven apparently healthy cats also went less than once a day, and nearly six in ten cats with CKD still went at least daily. The useful takeaway for peacemind is narrower: **a change from your cat’s own baseline, written down with dates, is more useful to a clinic than a vague sense that something shifted.**

| Read | What it usually means for you |
|---|---|
| **Routine / monitor** | Stable pattern matching your cat’s usual baseline; no other concerning signs — keep a simple dated count if you want a cleaner record |
| **Same-day** | A clear drop from *this* cat’s usual frequency lasting more than a few days, or combined with reduced appetite / drinking change — call your clinic today |
| **Urgent** | Straining, vomiting, off food, lethargy, or discomfort — do not wait overnight |
| **Emergency** | Collapse, severe distress, inability to urinate, or rapid decline — emergency veterinary care now |

Nothing below replaces a veterinarian’s judgment for *your* cat. Kidney disease is diagnosed with examination and lab work — never by counting at home.

## What one survey found

Jones and colleagues published a cross-sectional owner survey in the *Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery* (2021). Owners completed a questionnaire, then cleaned the litter box daily for seven days and reported what they observed.

The analysis included **124** owners of apparently healthy cats and **43** owners of cats with chronic kidney disease who submitted two or more days of daily observations.

Among apparently healthy cats:

- **85%** were observed to defecate one or more times per day
- **15%** went less than once per day

Among cats with chronic kidney disease:

- **58%** went one or more times per day
- **42%** went less than once per day

The difference in the proportion going less than once daily was statistically significant (P <0.0001). Observed frequency overall was also lower in the CKD group (P = 0.02). Observed fecal scores (consistency) were **not** significantly different between groups.

Full card with limitations and citation: [Cats with kidney disease went less often](https://www.pawai.it.com/research/defecation-changes-in-cats-with-kidney-disease).

## What this study cannot tell you

- It compared cats **already diagnosed** with CKD to others. It did **not** follow undiagnosed cats to prove that litter-box frequency detects disease early.
- The “healthy” group was owner-judged, not veterinary-confirmed — some may have had undetected conditions.
- Sample size in the CKD arm was modest (43 cats). Data were owner-collected at home without independent verification.
- Overlap is large: many CKD cats still go daily; some healthy cats do not. This is a **group difference**, not a test that sorts individual cats.
- It says nothing about urine volume, drinking, weight, or bloodwork — the tools clinicians use to diagnose CKD.

## What to note before you talk to your vet

If you track litter-box habits, track them so the clinic can use the note:

1. **Daily stool count** — written the day you clean the box, not reconstructed at week’s end.
2. **Baseline, not a rule** — a cat that has always gone every other day is different from one that used to go twice daily and now goes every other day.
3. **Duration** — how long has the change been going on?
4. **Multi-cat homes** — you often cannot assign stools to one cat from a shared box; say so rather than guessing.
5. **Companion signs** — appetite, water intake, vomiting, energy, weight — listed with dates.

For packing that into a visit story: [Why preparing before a vet visit helps](https://www.pawai.it.com/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-vet-visit).

## When not to “watch and log”

Straining in the box, repeated unsuccessful visits, vomiting, refusal to eat, obvious pain, or sudden collapse are **not** litter-box journaling problems. Contact a veterinarian promptly — or emergency care if your pet cannot urinate or is in severe distress.
