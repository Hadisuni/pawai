## The calm version first

Leaving a dog in a hot car is never OK. That message is right — and incomplete.

A large UK study of primary-care records found that **exercise** was the recorded trigger in most heat-related illness events that reached a clinic. Vehicle confinement was far less common as a trigger. That does not make cars safer per event. It means the walk is where most owners should keep their attention on a warm day.

PAWai’s job here is peacemind: separate **familiar campaign messaging** from **what the evidence shows about frequency**, so you know whether to change the walk plan, keep a dated record, or go now — without diagnosing heatstroke at home.

| Read | What it usually means for you |
|---|---|
| **Routine / monitor** | Cooler-day walks with normal recovery; no prior heat episodes — keep a simple warm-weather note in the pet record |
| **Same-day** | Recovery after walks is slower than your baseline, or the dog seems heat-intolerant in weather you’d once called “fine” — call your clinic today |
| **Urgent** | Heavy panting that will not settle, weakness, distress, vomiting, or confusion on a warm day — do not wait overnight |
| **Emergency** | Collapse, severe distress, inability to settle — emergency veterinary care now |

Nothing below replaces a veterinarian’s judgment for *your* dog. Heat-related illness can move fast.

## What one large study found

Researchers used the VetCompass programme to review de-identified records from **905,543** dogs under primary veterinary care in the UK during 2016. They identified **1,259** heat-related illness events across **1,222** dogs.

Among those events:

- **Exertion** was the trigger in **74.2%**
- **Environmental heat** (without exercise as the main label) in **12.9%**
- **Vehicle confinement** in **5.2%**

Brachycephalic (flat-faced) dogs had greater odds of all three trigger types compared with longer-muzzled dogs. Young male dogs had greater odds of the exertional type; older dogs and dogs with respiratory compromise had the greatest odds of the environmental type.

On outcome, the odds of death after a vehicular event were **not clearly different** from those after an exertional event in this data (the reported comparison did not reach statistical significance). So: cars are not “safer because rare” — they are simply a smaller slice of the events that showed up at clinics, while walks account for far more of the total burden.

Full card with limitations and citations: [Exertion caused far more heatstroke than hot cars](https://www.pawai.it.com/research/heat-related-illness-triggers-in-dogs).

## What this study cannot tell you

- It only counts events that reached a veterinary practice. Dogs that overheated and recovered at home — or died before arrival — are invisible here.
- It describes UK dogs in a single year (2016). Climate and exercise habits differ elsewhere.
- It does **not** define a safe temperature, duration, or activity level for any individual dog.
- It does **not** evaluate cooling or first-aid methods. Cooling advice has changed with newer research and should come from a veterinarian in the moment — not from a blog post.

A related VetCompass analysis of severe cases reported a **median ambient temperature of about 16.9°C** on days when severe events occurred. That is a good reason not to treat heat illness as a “heatwave only” problem.

## What to note before you talk to your vet

Most of the value is in what you change on warm days. A short record still helps the clinic:

1. **Prior episodes** — Has this dog struggled in heat before? At roughly what temperature and activity?
2. **Recovery time** — After an ordinary walk on a warm day, how long until panting settles? Is that getting longer across the season?
3. **Risk context** — Flat-faced, overweight, elderly, or known breathing problem? Write it plainly in the pet profile.
4. **Walk timing** — On warm days, note whether you shifted to cooler hours — and whether the dog still struggled.

Related reading on breathing noise that owners often normalize: [flat-faced breathing recognition research](https://www.pawai.it.com/research/owner-recognition-of-breathing-problems-in-flat-faced-dogs). ## Warning signs on a warm-day walk

**Stop, rest in shade, and call your vet for advice:** heavy panting that doesn’t settle with rest, lagging behind or lying down, heavy drooling, bright red gums.

**Go to a vet now:** collapse, wobbling or confusion, vomiting or diarrhoea, seizures, or gums that turn pale, grey, or blue.

**Before your next warm-day walk:** go early or late, shorten the route, bring water, and let your dog set the pace — with extra care for flat-faced, overweight, or older dogs.

## If you think your dog is overheating

Treat it as an **emergency**. Contact a veterinarian immediately and follow their instructions.

Do not use this page (or PAWai) for cooling steps, home treatment, or “wait and see.” Excessive panting that will not settle, distress, weakness, collapse, vomiting, or confusion on a warm day mean **call now**.
