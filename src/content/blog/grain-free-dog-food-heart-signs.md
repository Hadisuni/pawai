## A worry many owners carry quietly

You chose a grain-free food because it sounded like the better option. Then you read a headline linking grain-free diets to heart disease in dogs, and now every time you fill the bowl you wonder whether you've made a mistake.

That worry is understandable. The question has been public since 2018, and the evidence is still being assembled. One careful study gives a useful picture of where things stand, and it's less dramatic in both directions than most headlines suggest.

## What the research found

A 2021 study in the *Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine* examined **188 healthy dogs** of four breeds: Doberman Pinschers, Golden Retrievers, Miniature Schnauzers and Whippets. It compared dogs eating grain-free diets (**26 dogs**) with dogs eating grain-inclusive diets (**162 dogs**), taking age and breed into account.

- **Heart ultrasound (echocardiogram) showed no differences** between diet groups. Neither did NT-proBNP, a blood marker of heart strain.
- **Whole blood taurine was not different**, and plasma taurine was actually *higher* in the grain-free group (median 125 vs 104 nmol/mL).
- But **cardiac troponin I**, a blood marker that rises when heart muscle cells are damaged, was higher in the grain-free group: a median of **0.076 ng/mL vs 0.048 ng/mL**.
- Dogs whose food listed **peas, lentils or potatoes in the top ten ingredients** (39 dogs) also had higher troponin (0.059 vs 0.048 ng/mL), and more of them had irregular extra heartbeats during the exam: **10% vs 2%**.

The authors' conclusion is carefully worded: higher troponin in these healthy dogs *might* indicate low-level heart-muscle injury.

A separate 2018 case series looked at a different picture: **24 Golden Retrievers** already diagnosed with taurine deficiency and dilated cardiomyopathy. **23 of the 24** were eating grain-free diets, legume-rich diets, or both, and 23 of the 24 improved on ultrasound after a diet change and taurine supplements.

## What it can't tell you

The grain-free group was small, just 26 dogs. Dogs weren't randomly assigned to diets; their owners chose them, and owners who pick grain-free food may differ in other ways the study couldn't measure.

Troponin is a marker, not an outcome. A slightly higher average in a group of dogs whose hearts all looked normal on ultrasound is a signal worth studying. It isn't evidence that any particular dog will develop heart disease, and no dog in this study was followed over time.

Only four breeds were included, and the results may not apply to other dogs. The Golden Retriever case series had no comparison group and changed diet and added taurine at the same time, so it can't say which one helped. It also describes dogs that were already ill, which is a different situation from healthy dogs.

The honest summary: a link between some non-traditional diets and heart disease is plausible and under active investigation, but it isn't settled in either direction.

**Please don't change your dog's food based on this article.** Abrupt diet changes carry their own risks, and the right choice depends on your dog's breed, age and health. Take the question to your vet.

## Signs that can't wait

Whatever your dog eats, these are the signs that matter for the heart.

### Call a vet or emergency clinic now

- **Collapse or fainting**, even if your dog seems to recover quickly
- **Struggling to breathe**, breathing fast or with effort while resting or asleep, or breathing with the mouth open when they haven't been exercising
- **Pale, grey or blue-tinged gums or tongue**
- **Restlessness and being unable to settle or lie down**, especially at night, along with breathing changes
- A **suddenly swollen belly**
- **Coughing with breathing difficulty**, or coughing up frothy fluid

### Same-day or next-day vet

- A **new cough** that hasn't gone away, especially at night or when resting
- **Tiring much faster on walks**, stopping, or refusing exercise they used to enjoy
- **Weakness or lethargy**, along with eating less than usual

### Worth booking an appointment

- You're **worried about your dog's diet** and want to talk it through, ideally with your diet record in hand
- Your vet has **mentioned a murmur or irregular heartbeat** before and it hasn't been rechecked
- Your dog is a **breed with known heart risks** and you'd like to discuss screening

## What to keep in your dog's record

- **Exactly what your dog eats**: brand, product name and formula, not just "grain-free", with the date you started it
- **Every diet change**, with dates. Diet history is one of the first things a heart specialist will ask about
- **Treats, chews and table food**, which are easy to forget and can add up to a real share of what your dog eats
- **Exercise tolerance, coughing, fainting and breathing changes**, with dates
- **Any murmur or irregular heartbeat** your vet has noted, and when
